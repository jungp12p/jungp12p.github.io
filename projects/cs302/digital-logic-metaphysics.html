Digital Logic x Metaphysics

This project explores the intersection of digital logic circuits and philosophical questions about the nature of computers.

2-Bit Addition Circuit

Justification: This 2-bit addition circuit is constructed by combining a half adder and a full adder to correctly handle carry propagation between bits. The half adder computes the least significant bit using inputs X0 and Y0, producing a sum bit (S0) and a carry-out (C0). That carry is then passed into the full adder, which computes the most significant bit using inputs X1, Y1, and C0.

For X = 11 and Y = 11, the half adder receives X0 = 1 and Y0 = 1, producing S0 = 0 and C0 = 1. The full adder then receives X1 = 1, Y1 = 1, and C0 = 1, producing S1 = 1 and C1 = 1. The resulting sum bits are S1S0 = 10 with carry C1 = 1, yielding the binary result 110, which correctly represents the decimal value 6.

2-Bit Subtraction Circuit

Justification: The 2-bit subtraction circuit uses a half subtractor for the least significant bit and a full subtractor for the most significant bit in order to correctly handle borrowing across bits. The half subtractor computes the initial difference (D0) and borrow (B0) from inputs X0 and Y0. This borrow is then passed into the full subtractor, which computes the most significant difference bit using inputs X1, Y1, and B0.

Tracing the inputs X = 11 and Y = 11 illustrates the circuit's behavior. The half subtractor receives X0 = 1 and Y0 = 1, producing D0 = 0 and B0 = 0, as no borrow is required. The full subtractor then receives X1 = 1, Y1 = 1, and B0 = 0, producing D1 = 0 with no remaining borrow. The final difference is 00, which correctly represents the decimal value 0.

Reflections on the Nature and Structure of Computers

Our initial in-class discussions fundamentally reshaped how I think about what a computer is and how we decide what counts as one. The first question that challenged me was deceptively simple: Is a broken computer still a computer? Closely related was the question of whether a computer without power can still be considered a computer. My instinctive answer to both was yes. I believed that a broken computer remains a computer because it retains the potential to function again once repaired. I compared this to a broken piece of jewelry: even when damaged, it is still jewelry, simply waiting to be fixed.

When thinking about a computer without power, I drew a comparison to humans. Medically, a brain-dead person is considered dead, yet we still regard them as human. In the same way, I felt that a powerless computer does not lose its identity as a computer simply because it is inactive. In both cases, identity seemed to persist independently of function.

This reasoning was challenged by a counterargument raised by my classmate Adit. He pointed out that if we rely too heavily on potential, then almost anything could be considered anything else. A rock, given enough processing, could become part of a CPU—yet we would never call a rock a computer. Similarly, a group of books glued together would no longer meaningfully be a book. These examples forced me to reconsider whether potential alone is sufficient to define what something truly is. This tension between present structure and future possibility is something I still find myself thinking about.

The next major question we discussed was whether computers need to be useful. My initial response was yes. Computers compute, and computation itself felt inherently useful. Because of this mindset, I initially found the Tamagotchi reading somewhat silly. Although I understood that it was technically a computer, I struggled to see how it could be considered useful in any serious sense.

Over time, however, my understanding of usefulness began to shift. While a Tamagotchi does not optimize tasks or produce conventional outputs, it can still shape behavior by encouraging responsibility, attentiveness, and long-term care. This idea became clearer when my classmate London pointed out that usefulness is relative. The same object can be essential to one person and meaningless to another, yet it remains the same object. This made me realize that usefulness may not be an inherent property of a computer, but something assigned based on context and perspective.

The final question we explored was whether computers would exist without humans. My initial answer was no. I argued that computers and computation exist because humans invented the concepts that define them. Without humans to design, interpret, and understand computation, the category itself would not exist.

In response, my classmate Harry argued that while human-made computers might disappear, natural computation would still occur. Animals receive sensory inputs, process information in their brains, and produce behavioral outputs, which could be interpreted as computation. While this argument was compelling, I responded that although these processes would still happen, they would not be understood as computation without humans.

Another classmate, Joe, challenged this by suggesting that if we follow my reasoning, then nothing would exist without humans, since humans are the ones who classify and define reality. The fact that we can debate what computation is already assumes the existence of humans who define and recognize it. Without humans, physical processes may still occur, but computers and computation as conceptual entities would not exist.
